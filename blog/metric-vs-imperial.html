Metric vs Imperial: Which Countries Use Which System?

Almost every country in the world uses the metric system. Three do not, and the United States is the largest. Here is the full list, the political and economic reasons behind the US holdout, and practical advice for handling both systems when you travel, cook, or buy things online from another country.

The short version

Of the 195 countries in the world:

  • Ofﬁcially metric (or fully metric): 192
  • Ofﬁcially imperial / customary: 3 — the United States, Liberia, and Myanmar

That's it. Every other country, from Afghanistan to Zimbabwe, uses metric as the ofﬁcial standard. The US, by being the world's third-most-populous country (335 million people) and its largest economy, gives imperial an outsized presence despite being a global minority.

The three holdouts

United States. The US customary system (inches, feet, pounds, gallons, Fahrenheit) remains the dominant everyday system, though metric has been the ofﬁcial federal standard since 1893 (for trade) and 1975 (for general use). The US is the only industrialized country where you can buy a 2-liter soda, fill your car with gallons, weigh yourself in pounds, measure your height in feet and inches, and set your oven in Fahrenheit — all without seeing a metric unit outside the science lab.

Liberia. Liberia, founded by freed American slaves in 1847, inherited the US customary system and has never fully adopted metric, though metric is increasingly used in government, education, and the capital Monrovia.

Myanmar (Burma). Myanmar has used a hybrid of its traditional Burmese units and the imperial system inherited from British colonial rule. In 2013, the government announced a push toward metric, but adoption remains slow outside the main cities and exports.

The weird case of the United Kingdom

The UK is the country that invented the imperial system — feet, pounds, gallons, stone (14 lb body weight) all originated there. The UK officially adopted metric in 1965 and completed its metrication by 1980. But:

  • Road signs are in miles (distances) and mph (speeds).
  • Pint glasses in pubs are imperial pints (568 mL), not US pints (473 mL).
  • Body weight is still given in stones and pounds (e.g., "11 stone 4") in everyday speech.
  • People informally say their height in feet and inches ("5 foot 8") and baby weight in pounds ("8 lb 6").

So the UK is ofﬁcially metric but in practice lives in a hybrid world. This makes it the most useful case study: even after decades of official conversion, some imperial units stick around because changing them costs more than the inconsistency.

Why the US still uses imperial

There is no single reason, but a combination of factors keeps the US on customary:

Switching cost. The US has trillions of dollars of infrastructure labeled in imperial — road signs, factory tooling, residential blueprints, agricultural equipment, weather reporting. Replacing all of it would cost an estimated $10–20 billion and disrupt commerce.

Cultural attachment. The 1974 "Metric Act" was promoted as a federal switchover, but US public opinion was ambivalent. A 1975 Gallup poll showed only 45% of Americans supported metrication. By 2014, polls showed about 65% opposed full conversion. The "American exceptionalism" framing — "we are different, and that's fine" — keeps the issue politically untouchable.

Soft conversion has worked. The US has quietly become more metric over decades, mostly because the rest of the world insists on it for trade. Soft drinks are sold in liters, wine in 750 mL bottles, pharmaceuticals in mg, science in SI, athletic tracks in meters, car parts in mm. Only a handful of consumer-facing uses (gas, milk, body weight, road distance, temperature) remain stubbornly imperial.

The "no mandate, no transition" problem. Each US state can choose. Without a federal mandate, the cost of a state unilaterally switching (replacing all road signs, retooling factories) would not be recouped. Everyone is waiting for everyone else to go first.

How to handle both when traveling

Whether you are a US resident going abroad or a non-US resident visiting the US, a few habits save a lot of confusion:

  • Speed limits: 60 mph is 97 km/h. 100 km/h is 62 mph. Memorize 1 mph ≈ 1.6 km/h.
  • Distances: 1 mile ≈ 1.6 km. 100 km ≈ 62 miles.
  • Temperature: 20 °C ≈ 68 °F (mild). 30 °C ≈ 86 °F (hot). 0 °C = 32 °F (freezing). Memorize 20 °C = 68 °F.
  • Weight: 1 kg ≈ 2.2 lb. A "2 lb" bag of apples is just under 1 kg.
  • Volume: 1 L ≈ 33.8 US fl oz, or about 4.2 US cups. A US gallon is 3.785 L. A US "cup" is 237 mL; a metric cup is 250 mL.
  • Length: 1 inch = 2.54 cm exactly. 1 foot = 30.48 cm. 1 meter is a little over 3 feet (3 ft 3.4 in).

For a US resident in Europe, a 1-liter water bottle is your friend — every EU country sells them, the size is the same, and you stop having to think.

The future: will the US ever switch?

Probably not, at least not in any complete sense. The trend is continued slow conversion in background systems (science, manufacturing, automotive, pharmaceuticals) while everyday consumer measurements stay imperial. Most predictions for the next 50 years call for more hybrid use, not full metrication.

The "metrication" that did happen is irreversible: every new international product is labeled in both, every US textbook teaches metric, every STEM degree is fully metric. The US will be functionally metric in most of its economy while staying visibly imperial in everyday life.

Frequently asked questions

How many countries use the metric system?

192 of the 195 recognized countries officially use metric. The three that do not are the United States, Liberia, and Myanmar.

Why does the US use Fahrenheit instead of Celsius?

Daniel Fahrenheit created his scale in 1724, and the US inherited it from the British. Every other country that used Fahrenheit has since switched to Celsius. The US has not, mainly because the cost of changing all thermometers, ovens, and weather reports is enormous and the benefit is mainly international consistency.

Does the UK use metric or imperial?

Officially metric since 1965. In practice, a hybrid: road signs in miles, body weight often in stones, milk in litres but beer in pints. Most other measurements (food packaging, fuel, engineering) are metric.

Is Canada metric?

Yes. Canada officially switched to metric in 1975. Older Canadians sometimes still use imperial informally, but all packaging, weather, and road signs are metric.

Will the US ever go fully metric?

Probably not, at least not in the foreseeable future. The US is already metric in most of its economy (science, manufacturing, pharma) and will likely continue the slow consumer-facing hybrid indefinitely.
