Fahrenheit to Celsius: The Exact Formula and Quick Reference Chart

US weather reports, European ovens, Canadian recipes — temperature is one of the most common places to meet the imperial/metric divide. Here is the exact formula, a 20-row reference chart, and the mental math trick that works in your head.

The exact formula

There is exactly one right formula, and it has been the same since 1743:

°C = (°F − 32) × 5/9

To go the other way (Celsius to Fahrenheit):

°F = (°C × 9/5) + 32

Both are exact. The math is the same in both directions; only the operations flip.

Why subtract 32 first?

The two scales have different zero points. Fahrenheit set 0° at the temperature of a brine solution (the coldest thing Daniel Fahrenheit could reliably produce in his lab in 1724). Celsius set 0° at the freezing point of water.

So when converting, you first shift the zero point (subtract 32 to align 0°F with 0°C), then rescale (multiply by 5/9 because a Celsius degree is 9/5 the size of a Fahrenheit degree). Reversing those two steps gives the wrong answer.

A common mistake: people try °C = °F × 5/9 − 32. This is wrong. The 32 subtraction happens before the 5/9 multiplication, not after.

The −40° crossover

This is the most useful temperature fact in the world: at −40 degrees, Fahrenheit and Celsius are the same number. −40 °F = −40 °C exactly.

Why? Because the formula is °C = (°F − 32) × 5/9. Set °C = °F = x, and you get x = (x − 32) × 5/9 → 9x = 5x − 160 → 4x = −160 → x = −40.

It is the only temperature where the two scales agree, and it is a genuinely useful number to remember for any US weather forecast in northern latitudes.

Mental math trick

The exact formula needs a calculator (or a 5/9 multiplication in your head, which most people find awkward). Here is a fast approximation that is accurate to about 1°C:

  1. Subtract 30 from the °F (instead of 32).
  2. Halve the result.

So 80 °F: subtract 30 = 50, halve = 25 °C. True value: 26.7 °C. Off by ~1.7°C — close enough for "should I wear a jacket" decisions.

Going the other way: double the °C and add 30. So 20 °C: 40 + 30 = 70 °F. True: 68 °F. Off by 2°F.

Common conversions people get wrong

"Water boils at 212°F." True at sea level. At altitude it boils at a lower temperature — at 5,000 feet (1,524 m), water boils at 95 °C / 203 °F. Cooking recipes often assume sea level, which is why high-altitude baking requires adjustment.

"Body temperature is 98.6°F." That is an old average. Modern research puts it closer to 97.5 to 98.2 °F, and individual baseline temperature varies. The conversion to 37 °C is fine because 37 is just an integer rounding of the exact 36.85 °C.

"The oven is 350°F." That is a recipe convention, not a physics fact. The "right" oven temperature for a given dish depends on altitude, the oven calibration, and the dish itself. If a recipe calls for 350 °F and you only have a Celsius dial, 175 °C is close enough.

Frequently asked questions

What is the formula to convert °F to °C?

°C = (°F − 32) × 5/9. The 32 subtraction happens first, then the multiplication by 5/9 (or 0.5556 if you prefer decimals).

What is the formula to convert °C to °F?

°F = (°C × 9/5) + 32. Multiply by 9/5 first, then add 32.

At what temperature are °F and °C the same?

−40°. The only point where the two scales read the same number.

What is 100 °F in Celsius?

37.78 °C. That is a low-grade fever (normal body temperature is 98.6 °F / 37 °C).

What is 0 °C in Fahrenheit?

32 °F. The freezing point of water.

What is absolute zero in both scales?

Absolute zero is 0 K = −273.15 °C = −459.67 °F. It is the same physical temperature expressed in three different scales.
